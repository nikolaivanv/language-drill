## de-a1-accusative

- **Severity**: misleading
- **Confidence**: medium
- **Location**: what is it? — "German signals this case mainly through changes to the article; the noun itself does not change."
- **Problem**: Weak masculine nouns (n-Deklination) do change in the accusative, and several are A1 vocabulary: *Ich kenne Herrn Müller*, *den Studenten*, *den Jungen*, *den Kollegen*, *den Menschen*. A learner who trusts "the noun itself does not change" will write *\*Ich kenne Herr Müller*. Correct: the noun usually stays the same, except weak masculine nouns, which add -(e)n.
- **Source**: Durrell §1.3 (weak masculine noun declension); expert knowledge

## de-a1-imperative

- **Severity**: misleading
- **Confidence**: high
- **Location**: formation, callout "du-form spelling rules" — "**Optional -e ending.** Regular verbs may add a final -e (*Gehe!* or *Geh!*), but verbs with an e → i(e) change never take the -e." The table also says du = "stem only (drop -en, drop -st)".
- **Problem**: The -e is not optional for every regular verb. It is **obligatory** after stems ending in -t/-d (*Arbeite! Warte! Finde!*), after -m/-n following another consonant (*Atme! Öffne! Rechne!*), and after stems in -ig (*Entschuldige!*). The page presents "stem only" with an optional -e as the rule, which yields *\*Arbeit! \*Öffn! \*Entschuldig!*.
- **Source**: Durrell §10.2.1(f)(i) and (g)(iii)

## de-a1-modal-verbs-present

- **Severity**: error
- **Confidence**: high
- **Location**: common pitfalls — "**Adding können to perception verbs.** … The default is simply «Ich sehe das Meer.» (I can see the sea.) — not _*Ich kann das Meer sehen_ as a direct translation of the English pattern."
- **Problem**: *Ich kann das Meer sehen* is fully grammatical, idiomatic German. It is the standard way to say "I can see the sea" (from here), and the *können + sehen/hören* pattern is extremely common (*Kannst du mich hören?*). Marking it with an asterisk teaches learners that correct German is wrong. At most, *Ich sehe das Meer* is also possible. It is not the "default", and the modal version is not an error.
- **Source**: expert knowledge (Durrell ch. 15 uses *können* freely with perception verbs)

## de-a1-noun-gender

- **Severity**: misleading
- **Confidence**: medium
- **Location**: common pitfalls — "Nouns ending in -ung, -heit, and -keit are **always** feminine. There are no exceptions. If you see one of these suffixes, the article is _die_ — no need to look it up."
- **Problem**: The claim holds for the derivational suffix -ung, but a learner applies it to any noun spelled with final -ung. Common words like *der Sprung*, *der Schwung*, *der Ursprung* and *der Dung* are masculine (strong-verb nouns with no suffix). "No exceptions, no need to look it up" overstates the rule. It should be limited to the suffix -ung on verb-derived nouns (*Wohnung, Zeitung*).
- **Source**: Durrell §1.1.2 (lists *der Sprung* among suffixless masculine nouns from strong verbs; -ung in the feminine suffix table)

## de-a1-personal-pronouns

- **Severity**: error
- **Confidence**: high
- **Location**: formation callout — "The masculine accusative `ihn` is the only third-person singular form that differs between accusative and dative."
- **Problem**: This is false by the page's own table. All three third-person singular forms differ between accusative and dative: *ihn/ihm*, *es/ihm*, *sie/ihr*. (Only *wir/uns* and *ihr/euch* collapse accusative and dative, as the first half of the callout correctly says.) The intended point was probably that *ihn* is the only 3rd-singular accusative that differs from the **nominative**.

## de-a1-plural-formation

- **Severity**: misleading
- **Confidence**: high
- **Location**: when to use it — "Whenever a cardinal number greater than one precedes a noun, the noun must be in the plural"; pitfall — "The noun must always carry its plural ending when the quantity is more than one."
- **Problem**: Masculine and neuter nouns of measurement, weight and value stay singular after a numeral: *zwei Euro* (not *\*Euros* in prices), *drei Kilo*, *zwei Glas Bier*, *zehn Grad*, *zwei Paar Schuhe*. These are everyday A1 contexts (prices, shopping), and the numbers page itself uses *einundzwanzig Euro*. The "always" needs this exception.
- **Source**: Durrell §1.2.8

## de-a1-possessive-articles

- **Severity**: misleading
- **Confidence**: medium
- **Location**: when to use it — "**Replacing an indefinite article.** Use a possessive article wherever you would otherwise use _ein/eine/einen_ — that is, when the noun is not yet known to the listener or when you are making a general statement about possession."
- **Problem**: This confuses form with meaning. Possessives take the same *endings* as *ein*, but they are definite in meaning (*mein Auto* = the car that is mine). They are routinely used for things already known to the listener (*Wo ist mein Schlüssel?*). Saying they replace *ein* "when the noun is not yet known to the listener" is semantically wrong.
- **Source**: Durrell §5.2 (possessives share the endings of the indefinite article); expert knowledge

## de-a1-praeteritum-sein-haben

- **Severity**: error
- **Confidence**: high
- **Location**: what is it? — "Using the Perfekt equivalents («ist gewesen», «hat gehabt») sounds unnatural and is best avoided."; pitfall — "Saying «ich bin gestern krank gewesen» or «wir haben keine Zeit gehabt» is grammatically possible but sounds clumsy and unnatural. Native speakers consistently prefer war and hatte in all registers." Also the *es gab* example note: "the only natural option in this construction".
- **Problem**: This is overstated to the point of being false. Durrell gives *Vorige Woche bin ich in Bremen gewesen* as **equally frequent** as *war* even in North German speech. In South Germany, Austria and Switzerland the Präteritum is rarely used in everyday speech, so *bin gewesen / hab gehabt / hat es gegeben* are normal there. The accurate statement: *war/hatte* are much more common than for other verbs and safe to use everywhere, but the Perfekt forms are natural and common, not "clumsy".
- **Source**: Durrell §12.2.2(a)(i)

## de-a1-present-irregular

- **Severity**: misleading
- **Confidence**: medium
- **Location**: formation — "**werden** — the du-form loses the _d_, and the er/sie/es-form has a vowel change (e → i) as well."; example note — "werden: the er/sie/es-form is wird — both the d and the vowel change from the infinitive."
- **Problem**: The descriptions are garbled. Both *du wirst* and *er wird* have the e→i change, not just the er-form. *wird* keeps the *d*, and its actual irregularity is the missing -t ending (not *\*wirdt/\*wirdet*). "Both the d and the vowel change" is wrong about the d.
- **Source**: Durrell §10.2 (Table 10.5/10.17, *werden*); expert knowledge

## de-a1-temporal-prepositions

- **Severity**: misleading
- **Confidence**: medium
- **Location**: formation table row — "von … bis | dative | time spans"
- **Problem**: Only *von* governs the dative. *bis* is not a dative preposition: on its own it takes no article, and with time phrases it takes the accusative (*bis nächsten Montag*). Otherwise a second preposition sets the case (*bis zum Abend*). Labelling the pair "dative" leads to *\*bis nächstem Montag*.
- **Source**: Durrell §18.1.1

## de-a1-zero-article

- **Severity**: misleading
- **Confidence**: medium
- **Location**: common pitfalls — "Wir kaufen Äpfel., not *Wir kaufen einige Äpfel (unless you specifically mean «some, not all»)."
- **Problem**: *Wir kaufen einige Äpfel* is fully grammatical and simply means "we're buying a few/several apples". It is not an error, and *einige* does not mean "some, not all". Starring it teaches that a correct sentence is wrong.
